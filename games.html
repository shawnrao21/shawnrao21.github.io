Long-Distance Co-op

My friends are not in the same city any more, which rules out most of what Steam calls co-op. This finds the games we can actually play together, from different houses, on different machines, without all of us buying a copy. We call the result friendslop. It is affectionate.

The data

—loading

Every game here has at least 150 reviews, because a rating built on four of them tells you nothing. I rank by the lower bound of a Wilson confidence interval rather than the raw positive percentage, so a game at 5 out of 5 does not outrank one at 48,000 out of 50,000.

The part that took the work: Steam files four different things under "co-op". Online Co-op means you each own it and play over the internet. Shared/Split Screen and LAN mean you are in the same room or on the same network, which is no use to us. And Remote Play Together means one person owns it and streams it to everyone else, which is the one nobody knows about and the reason half this list is affordable. My first version collapsed all four into one flag and would happily have told us to play split-screen games from different states.

Those distinctions live in Steam's category data, not its tags, and the category data is localised per game: Counter-Strike comes back in Polish, Dota and Apex in Russian. So the build maps the phrases that matter across the languages actually present in the file. Where a game's mode still cannot be established it is marked unknown rather than guessed at, which is 190 of them.

      I represent each game as a vector of its eight rarest tags, weighted by how uncommon each tag is across the whole set, and take the cosine between two of those vectors. The weighting is what makes it work. Nearly everything is tagged Action or Indie, so without it almost every pair looks similar.
